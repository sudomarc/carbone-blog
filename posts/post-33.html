Congress Targets ASML’s China Engineers as the Chip War Moves to the Service Floor

"The proposed US restrictions on ASML's services could strain international alliances and accelerate China's self-reliance in chipmaking."

Carbone NotesPost 333 min read

The geopolitical competition over semiconductor manufacturing has long focused on the flow of advanced lithography equipment, but recent legislative action in the United States has shifted attention to the personnel who maintain that equipment. On November 27, 2026, reports emerged that Congress is pursuing restrictions on ASML’s China-based service engineers. The proposed measures, described by Crypto Briefing as part of an escalating chip war, aim to limit the ability of Dutch-origin equipment servicemen to operate within Chinese borders. The stated objective is to prevent the transfer of tacit technical knowledge that could accelerate China’s domestic chip-production capabilities, a goal that has driven export controls on the machines themselves for several years.

From Equipment to Personnel

ASML holds a near-monopoly on extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography systems, the machines required to fabricate the most advanced logic chips. For years, the company’s export licensing has been the primary lever used by the United States and its allies to throttling China’s access to cutting-edge fabrication. The new focus on service engineers represents a logical extension of that strategy: if the physical machines cannot be moved, the expertise required to keep them running and improving may be the next target. The Crypto Briefing report notes that the proposed restrictions could strain international alliances, particularly with the Netherlands, ASML’s home base, where policymakers balance commercial interests against security concerns.

The technical rationale is straightforward but carries significant diplomatic weight. Service engineers possess intimate, hands-on knowledge of machine calibration, maintenance schedules, and incremental performance tuning. Over time, this accumulated experience can bridge the gap between operating a machine and understanding how to build one from scratch. By restricting the movement of these engineers, the United States seeks to slow the pace at which Chinese foundries can internalize the nuances of high-volume manufacturing, even as Beijing pours resources into its own research and development.

Ripple Effects Across the Supply Chain

Analysts quoted in the Crypto Briefing article suggest that the move may accelerate China’s drive toward self-reliance, a goal explicitly outlined in the country’s five-year plans for the semiconductor sector. If service engineers are barred or heavily vetted, Chinese chipmakers may be forced to develop internal maintenance capabilities more quickly, potentially investing in training programs or recruiting talent from other regions. This shift could fragment the once-globalized ecosystem of semiconductor equipment maintenance, creating parallel tracks of knowledge and service that complicate future cooperation.

At the same time, the restrictions raise questions about the practicality of enforcement. ASML’s service model has historically relied on a distributed network of engineers who travel to client sites for installation, routine maintenance, and emergency repairs. Limiting this mobility could lead to longer downtimes for equipment, increased operational costs for Chinese foundries, and a potential slowdown in the production of chips used across a range of industries, from consumer electronics to automotive systems. The full economic impact will depend on how quickly alternative service structures can be established, whether domestically within China or through third-party intermediaries.

The legislative push also underscores a broader trend in technology policy: the move from controlling hardware to controlling the human capital that sustains it. As the chip war evolves, the boundaries of what constitutes sensitive knowledge are being redrawn. Service engineers, once viewed primarily as technical support, are now situated at the intersection of trade policy and national security. How this tension resolves will likely shape the next phase of the semiconductor competition, determining not just who owns the machines, but who understands them best.
